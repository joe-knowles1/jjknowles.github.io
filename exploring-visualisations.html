Exploring visualisations

26/11/2026

Before I start, let’s briefly recognise that there is a lot of cynicism around weeknotes, which is possibly fair enough. It can become somewhat performative and dry.

I think it’s different though when there’s thought and opinion put into them: when they become more blog style, rather than a bullet list. Widely popular are Mike’s — which give a sense of what he’s thinking about, rather than what he’s doing (often of course with overlap).

But who’s to say what’s right or wrong? If you want to publish a somewhat dry bullet list every week, do it. Everyone has their own reasons and trying to be open is A Good Thing.

There’s a personal benefit to writing it out I find, even if no one reads it. And I’m particularly keen on doing it for this period, as I started badly in screening and want to be more thoughtful and deliberate about my start in community.

In short, if you do come across any cynicism, I hope it doesn’t stop you.

-

ANYWAY.

I have been exploring different representations of our work this week, as we get something like towards the end of the first stage of our community discovery.

It would be much better to share the visuals for these but I can’t do that just yet.

Essentially we have pain points that spread themselves right across the journeys. Which makes you think that a linear representation isn’t ideal — some of the big issues sit in multiple places. The electronic patient record, for example, isn’t an issue in one place.

So I instead took the pain points and put them into four categories (all this inspired by Lou Downe by the way): data, people, technology, and places. The ‘materials’ of the service.

I plotted this on concentric circles and wrote out the affordances and constraints of each category.

What did this achieve?

Well, I think we will end up sticking with the linear, simply because of the narrative aspect it provides. It’s easier to understand.

But I think I’ve got a much better sense of the service now, and I think it’s much easier to see the problems for what they are. Community is unique in that there is no single healthcare setting — practitioners go out to people in their homes. So thinking about that aspect of it on its own is useful.

There are pain points in there for which there will be solutions, and that is so much easier to see in this format; when the pain point sits in the context of ‘assessment’ or ‘treatment’ in a linear journey, which has other aspects, it’s much harder to see.

Then again, when you isolate the problems, it will be harder to see the impact of the solutions on other parts of the service.

Will we use it? To be seen, but glad I tried it. I think it will be complementary. And it’s nice to step away from traditional mapping.

That’s all for this weak.
